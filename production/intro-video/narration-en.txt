00:00–00:17 | A picture is the beginning.
A partial differential equation can produce a beautiful animation. But what makes that picture useful for research? We need to see the model, check the calculation, and keep the evidence. This is PhaseAtlas: a visual workbench that connects those steps.

00:17–00:36 | Start with your equation.
Start with an equation and the question you want to investigate. In the Studio, researchers can edit the model, initial data, and boundary conditions. Supported models include coupled evolution equations and fourth-order problems. The numerical method and its limits stay visible, so users can choose an appropriate experiment.

00:36–00:58 | How quickly does a hot sample cool?
Here is a practical example: a hot rod losing heat through its ends. The Robin boundary condition models exchange with the surroundings. Watch the temperature fall, while the centre remains warmer than the edges. Every frame comes from a numerical solution. We can measure the mean temperature or follow a local probe. This dimensionless example helps explore heat transfer; a prediction for a specific material would also require calibration.

00:58–01:19 | Give the animation a numerical check.
A convincing animation is only the first step. We test the boundary implementation against a heat-equation solution that is known exactly. Keeping the time step fixed, we refine the grid from sixteen to thirty-two to sixty-four cells. The errors decrease, with an observed spatial order close to two. A separate balance diagnostic compares the change in thermal content with the recorded heat input through the boundaries.

01:19–01:40 | Turn one run into a research question.
Next, compare a family of experiments. This map asks how much heat remains after a fixed time, as diffusion and boundary exchange change. Every cell represents a completed numerical trajectory that can be replayed. Researchers can select their own observables. Failed runs and unobserved threshold events remain explicit, so the map does not silently hide missing evidence.

01:40–02:11 | From the original equation to new questions.
The platform also connects to my earlier research on a fourth-order travelling front. Here, we follow a small disturbance in a frame moving with the wave. Global, local, and weighted norms reveal different aspects of its evolution. The Studio supports two custom linear boundary conditions at each end for supported one-dimensional fourth-order equations. These tools help formulate and test research questions. A simulation alone does not prove nonlinear stability.

02:11–02:32 | Make the result useful to someone else.
The result is more than an animation. Researchers can export the model, parameters, trajectories, and numerical checks, alongside figures and video. Selected problems can be compared with an independent Python implementation. Computation runs on the user's device. That makes the workflow accessible for exploring a model, checking an idea, and sharing a reproducible calculation.

02:32–03:32 | A complete experiment, in one minute.
Here is the workflow in the live Studio. Start from a heat-transfer model, then edit the equation and its parameters. Type the spatial derivatives directly into the equation box. I will use diffusion on a one-dimensional rod, with heat exchange at both ends. Each boundary has its own coefficients, so we can change the environment without rewriting the solver. The left and right ends can also use different boundary data. Now run the experiment. Play the saved solution, pause it, or scrub through time to inspect a particular frame. Then choose Check resolution. The platform reruns the model with a smaller time step and a finer grid, and reports the differences. This comparison is something we can actually check. Finally, export the experiment as JSON. It includes the configuration, the saved fields, and the numerical checks, so another researcher can inspect and reproduce the calculation.
